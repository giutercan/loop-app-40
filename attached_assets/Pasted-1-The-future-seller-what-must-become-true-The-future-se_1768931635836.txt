1. The future seller (what must become true)

The future seller is not “more productive.”
They are more reliable under complexity.

Specifically, the seller shifts from:

Heroic execution → orchestrated execution

Personal intuition → evidence-informed judgment

One-off wins → repeatable value creation

That changes what deserves to be rewarded.

Managers are no longer rewarding:

Activity volume

Personal hustle

End-of-quarter recovery acts

They are rewarding:

Consistency of sense-making

Quality of customer confidence created

Discipline held when pressure rises

This is why the evidence pack is essential: it makes those invisible qualities visible.

2. What managers see in the Evidence Pack (their control panel)

Think of the evidence pack as a managerial stabiliser.
It answers one question continuously:

“Is this seller building trust in a way that will compound?”

A. Leading Evidence (behavioural signals)

These show before revenue moves.

Managers want to see evidence that the seller is:

Anchoring deals to a clear success frame (3–5 sponsor-owned KPIs)

Making customer assumptions explicit (what we think will move, and why)

Using method consistently (discovery depth, stakeholder coverage, deal shaping)

Translating insight into coherent narratives, not decks

Creating continuity from discovery → delivery → follow-up

These become observable patterns, not self-reported effort.

Examples of evidence objects:

Quality of success frame definition (clarity, sponsor alignment)

Deal narratives that link behaviour → system → KPI

Stakeholder maps that evolve over time (not static)

Follow-up loops that actually close (decisions logged, not just meetings held)

Managers don’t coach opinions anymore.
They coach artifacts.

B. Mid-loop Evidence (behaviour under pressure)

This is where trust is usually lost.

Managers look for:

Does discipline hold when the deal gets messy?

Do sellers revert to product pitching or stay outcome-led?

Are assumptions revisited when reality shifts?

Is AI being used to clarify judgment, not replace it?

Key evidence signals:

Revisions to success hypotheses (learning agility)

Quality of risk articulation (what could break this deal?)

Use of AI prompts and insights in-flow, not after the fact

Clean handoffs to delivery with explicit value logic intact

This is where future sellers differentiate.

C. Lagging Evidence (results, but with context)

Revenue still matters.
But now it’s interpreted, not worshipped.

Managers track:

Win rate movement by deal type

Deal size relative to success frame ambition

Cycle time with complexity context

Retention and expansion tied to original value logic

The evidence pack connects results back to:

Which behaviours mattered

Which interventions worked

Which patterns are reusable

This is what lets managers scale success instead of chasing anecdotes.

3. Metrics & rewards: how compensation evolves

Here’s the critical shift:

Rewards stop being a lagging scoreboard and become a reinforcement system for trust-building behaviour.

A. What stays (but is reinterpreted)

These don’t go away:

Revenue attainment

Win rate

Deal size

Retention / expansion

But they are no longer “raw.”
They are read through the evidence pack.

A miss with strong evidence ≠ a failure.
A hit with weak evidence ≠ a success worth scaling.

That’s a profound managerial shift.

B. What gets added (future-facing metrics)

These are not fluffy. They are managerially actionable.

Managers start to track:

Success frame quality index (clarity + sponsor ownership)

Deal discipline consistency (method adherence under pressure)

Evidence completeness score (can this deal be explained end-to-end?)

Trust velocity (time from first meeting to sponsor confidence)

Reusability index (can this pattern be reused by others?)

These are promotion and reward signals, not vanity metrics.

C. How rewards change in practice

Not overnight. Not radically. But directionally.

What must become true:

Variable comp starts to include quality multipliers, not just volume

Recognition shifts toward sellers who create repeatable clarity

Career progression favors orchestrators, not lone heroes

Managers are rewarded for building systems of performance, not just top performers

This aligns directly with what you and Jerry are framing:
trust as a system, not a trait.

The manager’s real payoff

With the evidence pack:

Coaching becomes precise, not generic

Performance reviews become grounded, not political

Rewards feel fair before the numbers fully land

Scaling success becomes possible

Without it:

Managers wait for lagging indicators

Sellers game activity

Trust erodes quietly

AI becomes noise instead of discipline
